01Partner Operations 101

Assume you know nothing. This is the whole idea.

What a "partner" is

Mistral could sell every model licence itself, with its own salespeople calling every bank and carmaker in Europe. It cannot afford to, and customers do not want that anyway. Enterprises already buy from someone: their cloud provider, the consulting firm that runs their IT, the software vendor whose product they live in. A partner is any company that helps Mistral reach, sell to, deploy for, or serve a customer, and gets something for it.

ELI5

You bake the best bread in town. You can sell it from your own shop, but you reach far more people if supermarkets stock it (cloud providers), if caterers put it in their menus (systems integrators), if sandwich shops build on it (software vendors), and if a delivery service carries it (resellers). Each of those is a partner, each wants a cut, and each needs to know your rules.

What "operations" means here

Partner Managers (the people being hired in London, Paris and Munich right now) own relationships. They meet Accenture every week, plan joint accounts, chase deals. Partner Operations owns everything those people need to exist but should not be building themselves:

  • The rules. What a partner has to do to join, what tier they are in, what they earn, who gets credit for a deal, what happens when two partners fight over one customer.
  • The systems. A partner portal (PRM), the CRM fields and workflows, the deal-registration form, the marketplace listings, the contract templates, the data feeds from Azure and AWS that say what was actually consumed.
  • The money. Margins, referral fees, rebates, market-development funds, credits, how invoices flow when Microsoft bills the customer and pays Mistral.
  • The enablement. Training paths, certifications, sales kits, demo environments, so that ten thousand TCS engineers can say the same true things about Forge.
  • The scoreboard. Partner-sourced pipeline, partner-influenced revenue, marketplace bookings, partner health, all in a form the CFO believes and the board can read.
ELI5

Partner Managers are the players. Partner Ops builds the pitch, paints the lines, keeps the rulebook, runs the scoreboard, and pays out the prize money. When something is unclear, Partner Ops is the referee.

Why a company at Mistral's stage suddenly needs it

Early on, partnerships are a handful of executive relationships and a spreadsheet. That works until the third time a Mistral seller and an Accenture partner both claim the same €4M deal, or Finance asks which of the "partner-influenced" numbers on the board slide are real, or a hyperscaler's monthly consumption report does not match Mistral's own invoicing. At that point the SVP hires someone whose job is to make the partner business boringly predictable. The "Head of" title means you are that person and you will build the team under you.

02Partner Operations 102

The mechanics you must be able to explain without notes.

The partner lifecycle

Every programme is a pipeline of partners, exactly like a sales pipeline of customers. The stages, and what ops owns at each:

  1. Recruit. Deciding which partners you want (a target list, not whoever applies) and the application flow.
  2. Onboard. Contract (the partner agreement and the partner-served deployment terms), portal access, NDA, tax and payment setup, tier assignment.
  3. Enable. Training, certification, sandbox access, sales playbooks, technical reference architectures.
  4. Activate. The first registered deal, the first marketplace transaction, the first joint customer. Most partners never get here. The ratio of onboarded to activated is the number that tells you whether the programme is real.
  5. Grow. Joint account planning, co-sell motions, quarterly business reviews (QBRs), incentives that reward the behaviour you want.
  6. Govern. Compliance, rules-of-engagement disputes, tier reviews, offboarding partners who do not perform.

Deal registration and rules of engagement

Deal registration is the single most important mechanism in partner ops. A partner tells Mistral, in a form, "we found this opportunity at HSBC for OCR on trade documents, roughly €800k." If Mistral approves the registration, that partner is protected: for a defined period they get the credit, the margin or the referral fee, and Mistral's direct sellers are told to work with them rather than around them. Rules of engagement (RoE) are the written policy for the edge cases: what if a Mistral rep was already in the account, what if two partners register the same deal, what if the customer wants to buy direct after a partner did the work.

Why it matters: without it, partners will not bring you deals because they fear being cut out, and your own sellers will resent partners because they fear losing commission. With it, everyone knows the price of cooperation in advance.

Sourced, influenced, and the attribution fight

  • Partner-sourced: the partner found the deal. Strictest definition, smallest number, most trusted by Finance.
  • Partner-influenced: the partner materially helped a deal that Mistral or someone else found. Broad, easy to inflate, and the number partner leaders love to quote.
  • Partner-transacted (or "through"): the partner is on the paper. The customer bought via Azure Marketplace or a reseller, whoever sourced it.

Your job is to define these so tightly that Sales, Finance and the SVP all agree on them before the quarter closes, then make the CRM enforce the definition rather than trusting people to tag deals honestly. A rule of thumb that survives scrutiny: sourced means the partner registered before any Mistral opportunity existed; influenced requires a named, logged activity (a workshop, a proof of concept, an architecture) before the deal reached a late stage.

Marketplaces and private offers

The hyperscalers run stores. Mistral's models are listed in Azure (Foundry), AWS (Bedrock), Google Cloud (Vertex) and IBM (watsonx). Two things make marketplaces strategic rather than just a listing:

  • Committed spend burn-down. Enterprises have pre-committed to spend, say, €50M a year on Azure. If they buy Mistral through Azure Marketplace, that purchase counts against the commitment. For the buyer it is "free" money they already promised to spend. This is why marketplace deals close faster and why cloud sellers care about them.
  • Private offers. A custom-priced, custom-termed deal, negotiated by Mistral but transacted and billed through the marketplace. Operations owns the mechanics: who creates the offer, how the discount is approved, how the marketplace fee and the payout timing are reconciled in Mistral's books.

Mistral's own partner-served deployment terms make the model explicit: when a customer buys through a cloud provider or reseller, that partner bills them, supports them, and is their "sole point of contact". Mistral supplies the product and the warranty. That is elegant legally and operationally painful: Mistral loses direct sight of the customer, so the data feeds from partners become the only truth.

Programme design: tiers, incentives, funds

  • Tiers (for example Registered, Select, Premier, Strategic) trade higher benefits for higher commitments: certified headcount, revenue targets, a joint business plan.
  • Margin and referral fees. Resellers buy at a discount and resell; referral partners get a percentage for an introduction. Both need a rate card and an approval matrix.
  • MDF and co-marketing funds. Money Mistral gives partners to run events and campaigns, released against a plan and claimed against proof. Ops owns the budget, the claims process and the audit trail.
  • Credits and funded POCs. The Microsoft deal explicitly includes Azure credits and funded proofs of concept. Somebody has to decide who gets them, track the burn and report the conversion.
  • SPIFs: short-term cash incentives to partner sellers for a specific behaviour, like the first ten Forge deals in DACH.

Enablement and certification

Accenture committed to training programmes and to equipping its own 784,000 people with Mistral. TCS is the first GSI on Forge. That means Mistral needs a curriculum, a certification exam, a way to track who passed, a demo environment that does not fall over, and a way to keep all of it current when the model line-up changes every few months. Partner Ops typically owns the system and the tracking; partner marketing and solutions engineering own the content.

Tooling

The stack you will be expected to have opinions on:

  • CRM (almost always Salesforce at this stage; confirm). Partner objects, deal-registration flows, attribution fields, partner-account hierarchies.
  • PRM (partner portal: Impartner, PartnerStack, Salesforce PRM; Crossbeam for account mapping). Where partners register deals, take training, claim MDF.
  • Marketplace tooling (Tackle, Clazar, Suger, or the native consoles) to list, price, create private offers and pull consumption and payout reports from the clouds.
  • Data and BI to reconcile partner-reported consumption with Mistral's usage and billing.
  • Contract lifecycle for partner agreements, plus legal templates for referral, resale and deployment terms.

The operating cadence

Weekly pipeline review with partner managers, monthly reconciliation with Finance, quarterly business reviews with each strategic partner, annual programme and tier review. The cadence is the product: it is how a partner business becomes forecastable.

05The hardest problems, and what a good answer sounds like

These are the things the interviewer is quietly worried about.

1. Channel conflict with a forward-deployed direct team

Mistral's growth so far came from its own engineers embedded with customers. Those teams will see partners as slower and less capable. Partners will see the direct team as competition. Good answer: clear RoE with a named deal-registration window, neutral compensation for direct sellers on partner-transacted deals so they do not fight the channel, an escalation path with a 48-hour SLA, and a published scorecard so conflict is decided by policy, not by whoever shouts loudest.

2. The hyperscalers are partners and competitors

Microsoft sells OpenAI, Google sells Gemini, AWS sells everything. Mistral's differentiation in those catalogues is sovereignty and open weights, not being the default. Good answer: treat each cloud as a separate programme with its own co-sell playbook, own the marketplace listing quality and private-offer speed as a competitive weapon (be the easiest model vendor to transact), and build relationships with the cloud's sovereignty and regulated-industry teams rather than their general AI sellers.

3. Money plumbing across six deployment paths

Cloud-hosted via a marketplace, sovereign cloud, partner-hosted, customer on-prem, air-gapped, on-device. Each has a different entitlement (who is allowed to run what), a different biller, a different support chain, and different revenue recognition. Open weights add a twist: a customer can download the model, so what exactly is being licensed and metered? Good answer: one entitlement model with a licence key or consumption meter that works everywhere, a monthly reconciliation of partner-reported usage against Mistral's own telemetry, and a decision rule for what is sold as software licence vs consumption vs support subscription.

4. Attribution the CFO believes

The company is headed for a $1B ARR claim and a possible public listing. "Partner-influenced" cannot be a vanity number. Good answer: definitions agreed with Finance in writing, enforced in CRM through required activity records, reported every month, with a partner-sourced figure that is deliberately conservative.

5. Enablement at GSI scale with a fast-moving product

Accenture alone has far more staff than Mistral has employees, by three orders of magnitude. Model names change quarterly and Forge is still early access. Good answer: a small, versioned certification with a clear owner, a train-the-trainer model inside each GSI, a sandbox with usage limits so it cannot become a cost centre, and a metric that ties certifications to registered deals rather than counting badges.

6. Sovereignty means bespoke everything

Outscale, S3NS, Scaleway and Proximus each need Mistral in a specific national or sector configuration. Every one will want custom terms. Good answer: a template sovereign-cloud agreement with a small number of negotiable variables, so the fifth one takes weeks rather than months.

7. Speed and headcount

A company of 800 growing revenue several-fold cannot wait for a 12-month programme build. Good answer: ship a minimum programme in 90 days on existing tools, and be explicit about what is deliberately deferred.

06How to portray yourself

The archetype, the vocabulary, and the traps.

The archetype: builder-operator

Not the relationship person (that is the Partner Managers), not the strategist (that is the SVP), not the pure Sales Ops analyst (too narrow). You are the person who has taken a partner motion from "a few executive friendships" to "a programme with a rate card, a portal and a monthly number", and who can do it again faster at a company moving at Mistral's pace.

Signal these traits

  • You start with the money flow and the data model, then the process, then the tool.
  • You have been the referee between Sales and Partners and survived it.
  • You quantify: activation rate, sourced pipeline, marketplace share of bookings, days from registration to approval.
  • You ship version one in a quarter and say what you left out.
  • You have worked with at least one hyperscaler marketplace and at least one GSI at programme level.
  • You are comfortable in a French-founded, European-first company with US ambitions: sovereignty is a real selling point, not marketing.

Avoid

  • Leading with "I love building relationships." That is the other job.
  • Proposing a big-bang PRM implementation before knowing what the field needs.
  • Quoting partner-influenced numbers without saying how they were defined.
  • Treating marketplaces as a listing exercise rather than a sales channel.
  • Sounding like you need a large team before anything happens.
  • Lecturing them about the AI market. Know the products; do not pretend to be a researcher.

Vocabulary to use naturally

Deal registration, rules of engagement, sourced vs influenced vs transacted, private offers, committed-spend burn-down, co-sell, joint business plan, QBR, partner tiering, rate card, MDF, SPIF, activation rate, PRM, account mapping, entitlement, consumption reconciliation, partner-served deployment, sovereign cloud, GSI, ISV, MSP, OEM, embedded, referral, resale. 11Glossary

Terms in the order you are likely to hit them.

GSI / SI
Global (or regional) systems integrator. Consulting and engineering firms that sell and build projects. Accenture, Capgemini, TCS, Sopra Steria.
ISV
Independent software vendor. A software company that embeds or integrates Mistral in its product. Snowflake, SAP, Cloudera.
MSP
Managed service provider. Runs IT for customers; can host and support Mistral on their behalf.
OEM / embedded
Mistral inside someone else's product, sold under their name. Different revenue and support terms.
Hyperscaler
Microsoft Azure, AWS, Google Cloud. Also a sales force and a store.
Sovereign cloud
A cloud operated under national or sector control so data and operations stay in jurisdiction. Outscale, S3NS, Scaleway.
Marketplace
The hyperscaler's store: Azure Marketplace, AWS Marketplace, Google Cloud Marketplace.
Private offer
A custom-priced deal transacted through a marketplace.
Committed spend
A customer's pre-agreed annual cloud spend. Marketplace purchases count toward it, which makes them easy to approve.
Co-sell
Mistral and a partner's sellers working the same account together, with agreed roles and credit.
Deal registration
A partner formally claiming an opportunity to be protected and paid on it.
Rules of engagement (RoE)
The written policy for credit, conflict and escalation between direct sales and partners, and between partners.
Sourced / influenced / transacted
Partner found it / partner helped / partner is on the invoice. Three different numbers.
Activation
A partner's first real commercial event: registered deal, transaction or joint customer.
Tier
A programme level with defined benefits and obligations.
Rate card
The published margins, referral fees and rebates by partner type and tier.
MDF
Market development funds. Money given to partners for marketing, claimed against proof.
SPIF
Short-term cash incentive to partner sellers for a specific behaviour.
PRM
Partner relationship management system. The partner portal.
Account mapping
Comparing customer lists with a partner to find shared and adjacent accounts.
QBR / JBP
Quarterly business review; joint business plan. The cadence documents for a strategic partner.
Entitlement
What a customer or partner is licensed to run, where, and how it is checked.
Partner-served deployment
Mistral's term: the product delivered through a cloud provider or reseller who bills and supports the customer.
Forward-deployed
Mistral engineers embedded with a customer to deploy and tailor the product. The direct motion partners are meant to scale.
Open-weight
Model weights are published; anyone can run them. Mistral monetises what surrounds the weights.
Neocloud
A newer cloud built for AI workloads. Mistral's term for itself after Mistral Compute.
Forge / Studio / Compute
Mistral's custom-model builder / application platform / European AI cloud.
